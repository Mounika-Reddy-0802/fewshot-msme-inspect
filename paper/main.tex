A Compute-Efficiency Benchmark of Frozen CLIP with Append-Only Memory}
\author{Mounika Reddy \and Ashi Sharma\\[2pt]
\normalsize [Department], [Institution]}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Small manufacturers need visual inspection that starts from a handful of photos
of good parts, runs on hardware they already own, and can take on a new product
without disturbing the ones in production. We benchmark a training-free
detector for this setting: a frozen OpenCLIP backbone, an append-only per-product
memory of patch tokens, multi-scale window scoring and optional fusion with text
prompts. On MVTec AD, VisA and NEU-DET, three backbones (ViT-B/32, B/16, L/14),
two precisions (FP32, INT8) and 1/5/10 shots over three seeds, we report
accuracy and CPU cost together, with every comparison paired. With ten good
photos the detector reaches 0.925--0.933 image AUROC on MVTec AD, statistically
level with PatchCore given the same photos, which localises better. The cheaper
ViT-B/32 matches B/16 when defects are large (MVTec AD, NEU-DET) and loses
0.05--0.09 AUROC when they are smaller than one of its patches (VisA). INT8
costs 0.005--0.02 AUROC and never changes which backbone to choose. Adding a
product is bit-identical to building it in from the start and leaves every other
product's scores unchanged. A laptop that runs at 37--88\,ms per image when cool
slows five- to six-fold under continuous use, yet still inspects 10,000 parts a
day for under Rs~41 of electricity a year.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A small manufacturer that wants automated visual inspection meets three
obstacles before accuracy is even a question. It has photographs of good parts
but almost none of defective ones, because a new line has not failed yet. It
has a laptop, not a GPU server or a machine-learning team. And its product mix
changes: a new part arrives, and the inspection system must take it on without
being retrained and without disturbing the parts already being inspected.

Industrial anomaly detection has largely solved the first obstacle by learning
only from normal images~\cite{bergmann2019mvtec}, and memory-bank methods such
as PatchCore~\cite{roth2022patchcore} reach near-perfect accuracy on standard
benchmarks. They do so with hundreds of normal images per product and report
accuracy alone. Vision--language models have since brought the number of images
down to a handful~\cite{jeong2023winclip}, but they are evaluated on GPUs, and
what they cost on the hardware a small manufacturer actually owns is not
reported.

This paper measures that cost. We study a deliberately simple, training-free
detector: a frozen CLIP image encoder~\cite{radford2021clip,cherti2023openclip},
an append-only memory of the patch features of a few good photos per product,
multi-scale window scoring, and fusion with text prompts. We report accuracy and
CPU cost together, across three backbones, two precisions and one to ten shots,
on three public benchmarks, with every comparison paired over three seeds, and
against PatchCore given exactly the same photos. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item a CPU-only benchmark of accuracy against latency, memory and FLOPs, with
  paired statistics and both burst and sustained-load latency;
\item append-only product addition, verified to be bit-identical to building
  the product in from the start and to leave every other product unchanged;
\item a rule for choosing a backbone that holds on three datasets: the smallest
  defect that matters against the image area one patch covers;
\item a like-for-like comparison with few-shot PatchCore, and a cost analysis
  showing that inspection on an existing laptop costs tens of rupees a year;
\item an open release with one command per table and a working demo.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Learning from normal images.} MVTec AD~\cite{bergmann2019mvtec} and
VisA~\cite{zou2022visa} established the protocol of training only on defect-free
images and testing on both. Embedding-based methods model the distribution of
features from an ImageNet-pretrained network: PaDiM~\cite{defard2021padim} fits
a Gaussian per patch position, and PatchCore~\cite{roth2022patchcore} keeps a
coreset of patch features and scores a test patch by its distance to the nearest
one. Our memory follows PatchCore's nearest-neighbour principle but stores every
support patch, since a few-shot memory is small enough not to need a coreset,
and keeps one memory per product so that products are independent.
Anomalib~\cite{akcay2022anomalib} provides the PatchCore implementation we use as
a baseline.

\paragraph{Vision--language anomaly detection.} CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip}
aligns images and text in one embedding space, which makes zero-shot detection
possible by comparing an image with prompts for normal and defective objects.
WinCLIP~\cite{jeong2023winclip} adds window-level features for localisation and,
with a few normal images, a reference memory (WinCLIP+).
AnomalyCLIP~\cite{zhou2024anomalyclip} learns object-agnostic prompts instead.
Both report accuracy on GPU; neither reports CPU latency, quantisation cost, or
what happens to existing products when one is added. We borrow WinCLIP's two
ideas, window aggregation and fusing text with the memory, but compute windows
from a single encoder pass and measure what each idea is worth.

\paragraph{Training-free adaptation.} Tip-Adapter~\cite{zhang2022tipadapter}
adapts CLIP to few-shot classification with a key--value cache of support
features and no training. We implemented its cache kernel as an anomaly head and
found that at its best setting it reproduces nearest-neighbour scoring exactly,
because the kernel is monotonic in cosine similarity and AUROC is rank-based; we
report this as a negative result rather than use it.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Encoder.} A frozen OpenCLIP ViT (OpenAI weights, QuickGELU
activation) maps a $224\times224$ image to a pooled embedding in the 512-dim
image--text space and to $H\times W$ patch tokens. We keep patch tokens at the
transformer width (768 for B/32 and B/16, 1024 for L/14) rather than projecting
them to 512: the unprojected tokens are $+0.020$ to $+0.026$ image AUROC better
(3.7--4.4$\times$ the paired standard error, 15 categories $\times$ 3 seeds). No
parameter is trained anywhere.

\paragraph{Append-only memory.} Adding a product stores the $\ell_2$-normalised
patch tokens of its $k$ good photos in a bank owned by that product alone. A
query patch's anomaly is $1-\max_j \cos(q, m_j)$ over the bank; the image score
is the maximum over patches, and the $H\times W$ map is bilinearly upsampled and
Gaussian-smoothed ($\sigma=4$). Because banks share nothing, adding a product
cannot change any other product's scores; we verify this with exact tensor
equality rather than assume it (Section~\ref{sec:incremental}).

\paragraph{Multi-scale windows.} Following WinCLIP's observation that defects
are judged better over a neighbourhood~\cite{jeong2023winclip}, but without its
per-window encoder passes, we average-pool the already computed patch tokens over
$2\times2$ and $3\times3$ windows, score each scale against an equally pooled
bank, spread window scores back over the patches they cover, and average the
scales. Scale 1 alone is exactly the memory above.

\paragraph{Text fusion.} Unprojected patch tokens cannot be compared with text,
but the pooled embedding can. The image score becomes
$(1-w)\,s_\text{memory} + w\,s_\text{text}$, where $s_\text{text}$ is the
softmax weight of a ``defective $\langle$object$\rangle$'' prompt ensemble
against a ``normal'' one; a typed defect description joins the defective
ensemble. The map is unaffected. We fix $w=0.5$, WinCLIP+'s equal weighting, and
report the full grid $w\in\{0,0.1,0.25,0.5,1\}$ rather than select on test data.

\paragraph{Deployment.} The visual tower is exported to ONNX (identical to
PyTorch on 50 real test images: patch cosine 1.00000, identical anomaly scores)
and dynamically quantised to INT8, which needs no calibration set. A Streamlit
application adds products from photos and an optional description, inspects
parts with a heat map, and keeps products on disk between sessions.

\section{Protocol}

\paragraph{Datasets.} MVTec AD~\cite{bergmann2019mvtec} (15 categories) and
VisA~\cite{zou2022visa} (12) with their pixel masks. NEU-DET~\cite{song2013neu}
has six classes of steel-surface defect and no defect-free images, so each class
in turn plays ``normal'': support photos come from it, and its test set is its
60 held-out images plus 60 drawn evenly from the other five classes (image AUROC
only; no masks).

\paragraph{Splits and statistics.} 1/5/10 normal support images, nested
($1\subset5\subset10$) so extra shots are measured against the same starting
images, on a fixed test set, for three seeds (42, 1, 2); all manifests are
committed. Comparisons are paired over identical (category, shots, seed) splits
and reported as the mean delta and its multiple of the paired standard error
(SE); we call an effect established above $2\times$SE.

\paragraph{Hardware.} One Intel i7-13700H laptop (6 performance + 8 efficiency
cores), CPU only. Latency is the median of 20 batch-size-1 passes after 5
warm-up passes at 6 threads (Section~\ref{sec:cost} explains why not 14).

\paragraph{Backbone choice depends on defect size.} On MVTec AD no B/16--B/32
difference reaches $2\times$SE (at 10 shots: $+0.000$ image, $-0.004$ pixel), and
on NEU-DET none exceeds $0.4\times$SE. On VisA, B/16 wins at every shot count
($+0.093$, $+0.050$, $+0.052$ image; 4.4--5.5$\times$SE). The reason is defect
size against patch size. A B/32 patch covers 2.0\% of a 224\,px image, a B/16
patch 0.5\%. Measured from the masks, a VisA defect covers 0.04--0.9\% of the
image; an MVTec AD defect averages 4.6\%; NEU-DET defect boxes average 39\%.
B/16's largest VisA gains are its smallest-defect categories. L/14 (0.4\% per
patch) improves on B/16 again on MVTec AD ($+0.030$ image, 4.6$\times$SE),
most on \texttt{toothbrush} ($+0.117$) and \texttt{screw} ($+0.086$); on VisA its
pixel gain is established ($+0.013$, 3.9$\times$SE) but its image gain is not
($+0.012$, 1.7$\times$SE), and on NEU-DET neither is (at most 1.1$\times$SE).

\paragraph{More shots buy predictability.} On MVTec AD with B/16 the mean
seed-to-seed standard deviation of image AUROC falls from 0.027 at one shot to
0.012 at ten; single-seed few-shot numbers understate this variance.

\paragraph{Windows help everywhere; text helps where ``normal'' means
something.} Windows add $+0.017$ to $+0.019$ image AUROC at 5 and 10 shots on
MVTec AD (3.3--4.3$\times$SE), $+0.016$ to $+0.021$ on VisA (2.7--5.5$\times$SE)
and $+0.044$ to $+0.049$ on NEU-DET (3.3--5.6$\times$SE, every class), where
every image is a texture judged better over a neighbourhood. They cost two
extra memory searches and no extra encoder pass, and leave pixel AUROC
unchanged (VisA: $-0.005$ at one shot). Text fusion helps most at one shot
($+0.016$ MVTec, $+0.018$ VisA, $\ge3.9\times$SE); text alone is far worse than
memory alone. On NEU-DET text \emph{hurts} ($-0.040$ to $-0.054$,
3.4--4.0$\times$SE): there the ``normal'' class is itself a defect type, so
``a photo of a crazing with a defect'' asks the wrong question. Fused per patch
in the 512-dim space, text also hurts (1-shot image AUROC 0.820 at $w=0$ falling
to 0.646 at $w=1$); fused per image on real products, it helps.

\paragraph{Against PatchCore with the same photos.} PatchCore is normally fitted
on hundreds of images; here it receives exactly our support photos, test images,
masks and metric code (Table~\ref{tab:patchcore}). At detection the two are
statistically level once our windows are on: no image-AUROC difference reaches
$2\times$SE on either dataset, and L/14 leads at one shot on MVTec AD
($+0.042$, 2.0$\times$SE). Plain single-pass B/16 trails on VisA ($-0.034$,
3.3$\times$SE), which windows close. At localisation PatchCore wins by
0.02--0.03 pixel AUROC (3--6$\times$SE); its $28\times28$ feature map is twice as
fine as B/16's $14\times14$, and only L/14's $16\times16$ draws level on VisA.
Per category the two have different strengths: ours on fine, textured defects
(\texttt{screw} $+0.120$, \texttt{grid} $+0.087$, VisA \texttt{capsules}
$+0.050$), PatchCore where a defect is a part out of place
(\texttt{transistor} $-0.173$, VisA \texttt{pcb3} $-0.115$). Per image the two
cost about the same on this laptop: PatchCore with 10 photos runs at 93\,ms and
0.6\,GB, our B/16 at 88\,ms, our B/32 INT8 at 33\,ms (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
The case for this detector is therefore not raw accuracy or speed but what
PatchCore lacks: products added in seconds without touching the others, typed
descriptions, and one backbone shared by images and text.

\paragraph{Against WinCLIP.} WinCLIP+ reports 93.1 / 94.4 / 95.2 image AUROC on
MVTec AD and 83.8 / 84.6 / 87.3 on VisA at 1 / 2 / 4 shots, means over five seeds
(\cite{jeong2023winclip}, Table~1), with a ViT-B/16+ backbone at 240\,px,
LAION-400M weights and one encoder pass per window. Our single-pass B/16 system
reaches 0.881 (MVTec AD) and 0.815 (VisA) at one shot. We did not reproduce
WinCLIP; across different backbones, weights and shot counts the comparison is
indicative only.

\paragraph{INT8 costs little and changes nothing structural.} Dynamic INT8 makes
every graph $4\times$ smaller and B/32 1.3$\times$ / B/16 1.8$\times$ faster
(Table~\ref{tab:int8}). It costs 0.005--0.02 image AUROC across both datasets and
backbones. Which backbone loses more is dataset-dependent (B/16 on MVTec AD,
neither clearly on VisA), and in no case does quantisation change which backbone
should be chosen.

\subsection{Adding a product}\label{sec:incremental}
Twelve MVTec categories form a deployed memory; three held-out categories (fixed
in advance as the alphabetically last) are appended from 5 or 10 photos. For
every seed and shot count: (i) the twelve existing products score
\emph{bit-identically} before and after; (ii) each appended product scores
bit-identically to a memory built with all fifteen from the start
(\texttt{incremental\_holdout\_*.json}). Appending a product end to end (reading
the photos, encoding, storing) takes 2.0--2.3\,s from 5 photos and 3.3--3.7\,s
from 10 on the idle laptop, almost all of it encoding, and 3.0\,MB (B/16, 5
shots); the memory work itself is 1.4--1.9\,ms per product.

Burst latency (torch FP32, batch 1) is 37\,ms for B/32 (3.0 GFLOPs), 88\,ms for
B/16 (11.3) and 397\,ms for L/14 (52.0); the memory search adds 0.3--2.9\,ms, about
1\%, so extra shots are effectively free (Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}). Peak RAM is
1.5\,GB for B/32 and B/16 and 3.7\,GB for L/14 in torch, which also loads the text
tower; the ONNX visual tower needs 0.64--0.67\,GB in FP32 and 0.38--0.39\,GB in
INT8. Under ten minutes of continuous inference the laptop steps down its clock:
B/32 holds 40\,ms for four minutes, then settles at about 230\,ms, $5.8\times$
slower; B/16 and L/14 run at 630--680\,ms and about 2\,s once throttled. Capacity
should be planned on the sustained figure. At 14 threads, timings on this hybrid
CPU were unusable (the same graph measured 36 and 526\,ms in consecutive runs);
at 6 threads repeats agree within 20\%. Under the same harness, PatchCore given
our 10 photos runs at 93\,ms (0.6\,GB); fitted on all 209 \texttt{bottle} images it
needs a 17.5-minute fit and runs at 170\,ms with a 131\,MB memory bank
(\texttt{patchcore\_cost\_i7\_*.json}). Assuming 45\,W and Rs~8/kWh, 10,000 parts
a day for 300 days costs Rs~9.80 a year with B/32 INT8 and Rs~40 with B/16 FP32;
even throttled, the laptop is busy at most 1.8 hours a day with B/16 (5.6 with
L/14). The study's benchmarks used at least 6.8 CPU-hours and no GPU; hardware
spend was Rs~0 (\texttt{cost\_feasibility.json}).

\subsection{Verification}
Each headline number was checked beyond the run that produced it. Re-extracting
every feature from raw images reproduces all 135 MVTec AD (B/16) and 108 VisA
(B/32) result rows to within 0.0002. NEU-DET was produced on one member's
machine and reproduced on the other's: the split manifests regenerate
byte-identically, and every per-class AUROC matches to within 0.0005. A fresh
clone installed into a new environment from the pinned requirements passes all
193 tests. The architecture was frozen before the final experiments, and a test
fails if any frozen model file changes.

\section{Limitations}
The datasets are public benchmarks under controlled capture, not a shop floor.
Thin defects remain below the patch resolution (\texttt{screw}: 0.792 at 10
shots even with L/14), and PatchCore's finer feature map localises better. Image
scores still confuse tiny defects with normal variation on VisA (0.855 best)
while maps localise well (pixel $\ge$0.95). With only three photos, the
leave-one-out threshold the application suggests is too cautious and can miss
defects; five photos or a hand-set threshold avoid this. We did not reproduce
WinCLIP or AnomalyCLIP, test static INT8, or evaluate MPDD.

\section{Conclusion}
A frozen CLIP encoder with an append-only memory inspects parts from ten good
photos at 0.925--0.933 image AUROC on MVTec AD on a laptop CPU, level with
PatchCore given the same photos, and adding a product changes nothing about the
products already in place, a property we verify exactly rather than
approximately. The cost picture is more useful than any single latency: the
cheapest backbone is enough when defects are large but not when they are smaller
than its patches, a rule that held on all three datasets; INT8 trims 0.005--0.02
AUROC for a 1.3--1.8$\times$ speed-up without changing that choice; and a laptop
that runs at 37\,ms per image when cool settles at five to six times that under
continuous use. Even at the throttled rate, a laptop the workshop already owns
inspects 10,000 parts a day with hours to spare, for tens of rupees of
electricity a year. The open questions are the ones public benchmarks cannot
answer: defects thinner than a patch, and the gap between benchmark photographs
and a shop floor.
